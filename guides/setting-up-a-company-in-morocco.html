Setting up a company in Morocco: the foreign investor's checklist

By the 00212 Editorial Desk · Updated 2026-09-28 · 6 min read

General information only. Company law and tax rules change. Confirm with a Moroccan lawyer or your Regional Investment Centre (CRI) before acting.

1. Choose a legal form

FormBest forNotes
SARL (limited liability company)SMEs, trading, servicesMost common; flexible; one or more partners
SA (joint-stock company)Large industrial projectsBoard governance, auditor; higher capital requirements
Branch (succursale)Testing the marketNo separate legal personality; parent is liable
Liaison officeRepresentation onlyCannot trade

2. Registration steps

  1. Negative certificate (name reservation) from OMPIC.
  2. Statutes drafted and signed, plus a capital deposit where required.
  3. Registrations: trade register (RC), tax identifier (IF), business tax, the ICE common business identifier, and social security (CNSS). Many of these can be done through the CRI one-stop shop.
  4. Bank account and foreign-exchange formalities for capital coming from abroad, so that profits can be repatriated later.
  5. Sector licences and admission to an industrial acceleration zone if you need one.

3. Taxes at a glance

  • Corporate income tax: Morocco has been moving to unified rates, with a standard rate of about 20% and a higher rate for very large profits. Zone and export incentives may apply.
  • VAT: standard rate 20%, with reduced rates for some goods and services.
  • Withholding tax on dividends and some cross-border service payments. Check the China–Morocco double-tax treaty.

4. Realistic timeline

A straightforward SARL can be registered in a few weeks when documents are ready. Foreign documents need apostille or legalisation and translation into French or Arabic, which adds time. Industrial projects that need zone admission, land and environmental permits usually take several months.

5. Common pitfalls

  • Using untranslated or unlegalised Chinese documents.
  • Not registering foreign capital correctly, which complicates repatriating dividends later.
  • Underestimating the need for French in administration.
  • Hiring foreign staff before work-permit approval.
